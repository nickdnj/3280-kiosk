## The window is now smaller than the picture

Until Rev 2b the window was cut **generous** — a quarter inch of slack per side
that landed on the monitor's own black bezel. Black on black; nothing shows.

That was wrong, and it is the kind of wrong you only see once it is built. A
bezel is not the same black as a matte ACM panel, it has a different sheen, it
usually carries a moulded logo and a power LED, and there is always a shadow
line at the transition. What you would have got is a monitor visible inside a
hole. What a museum instrument looks like is **illuminated LCD in a crisp routed
opening, and nothing else.**

So the rule is inverted. **The window is cut 3/16″ smaller than the lit
rectangle in each direction** — the ACM masks the outer **3/32″ of picture on
every side** and the bezel never appears at all.

The masked strip is about **8 pixels** of 1080 per side. At three to six feet
that is invisible; the kiosk UI has margins far larger than that anyway.

### What it costs

The face plate is no longer independent of the monitor. It used to be safe to
cut before the monitor arrived, because the monitor was aligned to the window by
the internal VESA frame. Now the window is derived *from* the monitor, and the
derivation is unforgiving: across the range of panels sold as "24 inch", the
right window height varies by **0.35″** — four times the whole overlap. Cutting
this window for the wrong panel either exposes bezel or crops visibly.

**So the monitor gate now comes first.** It always was first in the build
sequence; it just didn't block this part before.

### Measure it like this

1. Power the monitor. Display a **full-screen white image**.
2. Measure the **lit rectangle** with a tape — not the advertised diagonal, not
   the outside of the casing.
3. Put the two numbers into `ACT_W` / `ACT_H` in `_p1.py`, set
   **`ACT_MEASURED = True`**, and re-run. The window follows automatically,
   rounded to land on tape marks.

Until that flag is set, `make-cutfiles.py` stamps the SVG **NOT FOR RELEASE**
and prints a banner. That is deliberate.

Step **0** of the build cookbook is this measurement, with a drawing.

## The light seal — and why there is no shroud

Behind the plate, around the window, goes **1/4″ wide × 3/16″ thick black
closed-cell neoprene foam tape**, set **1/8″ out from the window edge**. About
65″ of it.

Its only job is to kill the light leak and the shadow line between the ACM and
the bezel. It is **not a gasket and not structure**:

- The **VESA rails set the monitor's position** — 1/8″ back from the ACM. The
  foam is 3/16″ uncompressed, so it lands at **33 % compression**: enough to
  seal, nowhere near enough to push the panel.
- **Never let the plate clamp the glass.** If the foam is doing work, the
  monitor is in the wrong place; move the rails, not the plate.
- **The foam must never touch the LCD.** The 1/8″ inset guarantees that: the
  picture ends 3/32″ out from the window edge, so the foam starts 1/32″ beyond
  the glass and lands wholly on the bezel. `_p1.py` checks both edges.

**No shroud.** A hood behind the window would add parts, collect dust and make
service worse, and it buys nothing a 1/8″ setback and a black plate don't
already give. Against a matte black face the opening reads as a deep black
window on its own.

## ⚠ And still confirm this number

**⌀30.5 mm is the cutout for a "30 mm" anti-vandal switch.** Most want
30.2–30.5 mm and cutting the larger size is the safe direction — the nut and
washer cover the slack. **But this is a one-piece part now, so a wrong cutout
means recutting the whole face.** Check it against the datasheet of the switch
you are actually buying, along with:

- **panel thickness range includes 3 mm**
- **actuation force ≤ 5 lbf** (ADA §309.4)

If your switch wants a different cutout, change `BTN_HOLE` in the script and
re-run.

**Do not order ahead of this check.** There are now **two** gates on P1: the
switch cutout above, and the measured lit rectangle. Both are cheap to clear and
neither is recoverable after the part is routed.

## Why one piece

An earlier version split this into a face plate plus a separate button plate.
That was over-engineered. The separate plate bought insurance against the switch
cutout, and paid for it in cost, stiffness and assembly:

- Cutting both a 12.17 × 21.24 window **and** an 11.87 × 3.00 button aperture out
  of one 3 mm sheet left a **1.35″ web** as the only thing joining the side rails
  below the screen. One piece leaves **2.25″** above the buttons and **2.65″**
  below — a much stiffer part.
- One part on the invoice instead of two, six fewer holes, no second set of
  cleats, and no plate to align square in an aperture.
- The wear argument didn't hold up: anti-vandal switches are rated for millions
  of cycles, and when one fails you replace **the switch**, through the same hole.

The insurance is better bought by **confirming the cutout before ordering** than
by designing around not knowing it.

## What the drawing assumes

- **The button centreline lands 26″ below the top edge.** With the kiosk bottom
  at **34″ AFF** that puts the buttons at **38″ AFF** — the ADA datum the whole
  design is placed from. Both numbers are now whole. Don't change the plate
  height without redoing that arithmetic; `_p1.py` checks it on every run.
- **Mount holes moved from 5/8″ to 1/2″ in from the edge.** At 5/8 a 0.375″
  brass insert broke out of a 3/4″ board's front edge. At 1/2 it spans
  0.3125–0.6875 and sits entirely inside. That was a real defect, not a tidy-up.
- **The screen window is deliberately undersize** — it laps 3/32″ over the
  picture on every side, so no bezel is visible. See above. This is the change
  that made P1 depend on the monitor.
- **The window is centred on the plate, so the picture is too — and the casing
  therefore is not.** In portrait, a monitor's thick "chin" bezel ends up on a
  **side** edge, and centring the picture shifts the casing sideways by half the
  chin-to-thin difference. On the nominal monitor that is 0.248″, which leaves
  only **1/16″** between the casing and the cavity wall on the chin side.
  It fits, but it is the tightest number in the box, and it turns the chin into
  a buy criterion: **chin ≤ 0.92″**. `make-cutlist.py` checks it.
- **The cleat row** — three holes between the window and the buttons — lands on a
  plywood rail that takes the press load, so the panel doesn't flex when a
  visitor pushes a button. It sits above the switch bodies, which protrude about
  an inch behind the plate.

## Cardboard mock-up — print it 1:1 first

**[`P1-face-plate-stencil.pdf`](P1-face-plate-stencil.pdf)** tiles this part
across **eight US Letter pages at full size**, plus an instruction sheet. Print it, tape the sheets on the
registration marks, cut the voids, and trace the shape onto cardboard.

**Print the PDF, not the HTML.** The page box is embedded at exactly 8.5 × 11,
so "Actual size" prints true 1:1. Printing HTML from a browser is where scale
errors come from. [`stencil-letter.html`](stencil-letter.html) is the source.

1. Print at **Actual size / 100%** — *not* "Fit to page" — and **Margins: None**.
2. **Measure the red bar on sheet 1. It must be exactly 4.000 inches.** If it
   isn't, the printer scaled the page; nothing downstream will line up.
3. Tape on the registration crosses in the shaded overlap bands (0.80″
   horizontal, 3.20″ vertical). The 1″ grid is the real check — lines must run
   straight across every joint and the printed `x,y` coordinates must agree on
   both sides of the tape.
4. Cut the heavy dashed outlines (window and buttons). The small circles are
   mounting holes — **mark only**. Cut the outside profile **last**.

Hold the cardboard against the 3280 with its **bottom edge 34″ above the
floor**; that puts the button centres at 38″ AFF. Proportion is the argument
this design turns on, and a photograph of cardboard on the real machine settles
it in a way no drawing can. Take one straight on from six feet and one from
about 45°.

Regenerate with `python3 make-stencil.py`.

## Labels

There is no text in the DXF, and **SendCutSend does not engrave ACM** — routing
is the only service they offer on it. So lettering is a separate step, and the
native answer is **cut vinyl from a sign shop**: this is sign material, and it is
how every road sign is lettered. Durable, cheap, and replaceable when a docent
decides BACK should have said PREVIOUS.
