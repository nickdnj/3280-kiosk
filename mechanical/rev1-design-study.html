The kiosk is its own product

A self-contained enclosure — display, Pi, power, three buttons — built and bench-tested on a table, then hung on the front of the 3280's closed factory door. The machine is not opened, not drilled, not modified. The mount is a separate subsystem, designed later.

Display size

The 3280 stays visually dominant. That is the whole design constraint of this view: a visitor should read a historic minicomputer with an interpretation device added to it, not a monitor wearing a cabinet.

Watch what happens at 32″. Its top clears the door line, and it takes up half the machine's face. The geometry technically resolves if the buttons drop to about 38″ — the proportion does not.

The enclosure by itself. Everything in this drawing is buildable and testable before the 3280 is involved at all.

Sheet R1-02 · kiosk front elevation
Sheet R1-03 · button plate detail

Why a separate button plate

The buttons are the highest-wear part of the exhibit and the only part a visitor touches. Splitting them onto their own plate decouples switch-hole tolerance from the big face plate, lets the whole control assembly be built and tested on the bench with a jumper to the Pi, and means a change of switch style recuts a $15 plate instead of the face.

30 mm anti-vandal stainless, momentary, 30.5 mm hole. A big public target, industrial life, about $10 each, and a hole size so standard that any replacement in 2033 will fit. Labels engraved into the plate — stickers peel.

Flat, not angled. An angled lower surface adds a bevelled joint, adds depth at the bottom, and complicates the mount's load path. At 40″ AFF a flat face is comfortable for a standing adult. Revisit only if observation says otherwise.

No fasteners on the visitor-facing surface. The plate is captured from behind — nothing on the front is a screw a bored twelve-year-old can turn.

03Side section

Depth is the number that decides how much the kiosk stands off the machine. Every eighth of an inch here is visible.

Sheet R1-04 · side section · depth to 3.5″ target · mounting interface left blank by intent

What forces the depth

  • The monitor's thickest point — 1.6″ to 2.3″. The only hard number in the stack, and the reason depth grows with screen size even though the panel doesn't.
  • Cable bend at the connectors — 0.75″ to 1.75″. The one you can design away, and the difference between 3.5″ and 4.5″. Route HDMI and DC downward into the button bay with right-angle plugs.
  • Face plate + rear panel — 1.0″ combined. Down to ~0.4″ with composite panel, at the cost of stiffness.

What does not

  • The Raspberry Pi. 0.65″ tall, and it does not go behind the monitor. It goes in the button bay on a slide-out tray, where 4″ of depth is otherwise doing nothing. Design the enclosure around fitting a Pi behind the panel and you will build it an inch too deep.
Reversed from earlier work

The monitor stays cased. De-casing buys about 1.2″ and costs the EMI shielding, the thermal design, the power board's own enclosure, and any hope of a volunteer replacing it. Not worth it.

Readability governs, resolution doesn't

The real constraint is cap height. Comfortable reading at 6 ft wants roughly 0.35″ of cap height — about a half-inch em. On the 24″, 20.74″ tall across 1920 px, that is a 46 px body size, giving about 47 characters per line. A good measure for the 3–5 bullets per screen the content bar allows.

The 22″ lands at ~42 characters. Workable, but it starts to constrain the writing. All four are legible at 3–6 ft; a 1080p 24″ at 93 ppi is crisp at arm's length and dead sharp at exhibit distance. Don't pay for 1440p unless the salvage pile hands it to you.

Recommendation — B

The single biggest risk in a volunteer-built enclosure is the finish. A well-built box with a brushed-lacquer face still looks homemade, and that is exactly the failure mode this design is trying to avoid. Approach B takes the finish off the critical path: the visible surface arrives already flat and already durable, and the volunteers build a plywood box behind it — a thing any woodworker gets right on the first try.

A is the fallback if nobody can source or bond sheet facing. It's a good enclosure; it just puts the project's appearance on the line at the spray stage. C is worth ten minutes of searching and not worth designing around — nothing off the shelf is 15″ × 29″ × 3.5″ portrait.

Serviceability

The rule: nothing that fails routinely should require touching the face plate or the mount. The kiosk should never have to come off the 3280 for service.

PartHow it comes out
Rear panelCaptive thumbscrews or quarter-turn fasteners into threaded inserts. No loose hardware to drop inside a museum artifact.
MonitorOn a VESA 100 plate bolted to the internal frame. Rear panel off, four bolts, monitor out backwards. The face plate never comes off.
Raspberry PiSlide-out tray in the button bay. Unplug two, slide out.
ButtonsPlate captured from behind. Rear panel off, plate drops in. Standard 30.5 mm hole.
PowerOne appliance inlet at the bottom rear. One cord leaves the kiosk.

06Attachment interface

Deliberately not designed. The enclosure's job here is to have a flat back and a known weight — that is enough for an adapter to be designed against later, and designing it now would be guessing.

Sheet R1-05 · rear · mounting interface zone, design TBD

Constraints recorded now, so the enclosure doesn't foreclose them

  • Reversible, non-destructive, removable, visually discreet. No holes, no cuts, no permanent modification to the door or the cabinet. This is a hard requirement, not a preference — the 3280 is a museum artifact.
  • Must carry the kiosk in shear plus the moment from a centre-of-mass roughly 1.75″ off the door, with a safety factor for someone leaning on it.
  • Candidates, unevaluated: edge brackets hooking the door's return flange; compression straps around the cabinet; a full-door backing plate captured top and bottom; clamps on existing door hardware.
  • Anything that loads the original hinges has to be checked against them.
This is why weight matters

The mount is undesigned and unconstrained. 23 lb of strap-and-bracket load on a hollow sheet-metal door is a solvable problem. 34 lb is where you start having to transfer load to the cabinet frame — and the mount stops being simple. That, more than proportion, is the argument against the 32″.

07Recommendation

Rev 1

24″-class display, 23.8″ actual. Finished kiosk 15-3/8″ W × 28-11/16″ H × 3-1/2″ D, about 23 lb.

Proportion is the deciding argument. At 15.4″ wide the kiosk is 63% of the door — clearly an added object, clearly not trying to be the machine. At 70% and 79% the 27″ and 32″ start to read as a monitor wearing a cabinet, which is precisely the impression a museum exhibit should not give.

Weight is the second argument, and it may be the more important one — see section 06. The mount has to carry this off a hollow door without a single fastener into the artifact.

Availability closes it. 24″ 1080p IPS is the highest-volume monitor ever made. It is the most likely thing in the warehouse, the cheapest thing to buy, and the easiest thing for a docent to replace in 2033 without calling anyone.

If a 27″ falls out of the salvage pile

Take it. It isn't a bad answer — it's a slightly worse answer, and the enclosure is parametric enough that switching costs one face plate. Budget the extra 4 lb into the mount. 22″ is the fallback if the mount turns out harder than expected; 20 lb is genuinely easy to hang. 32″ is out — height failure at a comfortable button position, the weight, and the VA-panel risk, since visitors approach an exhibit from the side and a VA panel at 45° looks bad in exactly that situation.

Two go/no-go criteria on any candidate monitor

1 · It must power itself back on after a mains cut. The exhibit runs on an AC timer. Many monitors return to standby and need a button press — that's a black screen every morning until a docent notices. Test before committing: switched strip, kill power, restore, confirm it comes back with no input.

2 · Matte only. Overhead lights and daylight. Glossy panels mirror them.

Sequence

  1. Choose the monitor. Buy or salvage; run the power-cut test. Gate — everything downstream is dimensioned off this.
  2. Build the enclosure. Bench, no 3280 involved.
  3. Install monitor, Pi, buttons, wiring, software.
  4. Bench-test as a complete kiosk. Run it for a week on a table.
  5. Then design and build the reversible mounting adapter.
  6. Rev 2, if desired, revisits the replacement-door concept.

Steps 1–4 have no dependency on the 3280 at all. C1 — the closing clearance that gated the entire previous design — no longer gates anything, and neither does the door height, the aperture, or the inner perforated panel.
