The kiosk is its own product

A self-contained enclosure — display, Pi, power, three buttons — built and bench-tested on a table, then hung on the front of the 3280's closed factory door. The machine is not opened, not drilled, not modified. The buttons are the datum: ADA reach sets their height, and the height sets where everything else goes.

The 3280 stays visually dominant. That is the whole design constraint of this view: a visitor should read a historic minicomputer with an interpretation device added to it, not a monitor wearing a cabinet.

The button line is the datum. The kiosk is placed from the bottom up: the buttons go where a hand can reach them, and the screen goes wherever that leaves it. Section 02 sets that height from the ADA reach range; the shaded band on the drawing is the legal 15″–48″ window.

Push the button slider up and the 32″ runs out of door before the others do — that constraint, not screen area, is what separates the sizes.

02Button height drives placement

The buttons are the datum. Where a visitor's hand goes decides where the kiosk sits, and everything above it follows. Move the slider — the elevation, the fit check and the table all follow the button line.

The rule

ADA 2010 §308 puts operable parts between 15″ and 48″ above the finished floor. Unobstructed forward reach and unobstructed side reach give the same 48″ maximum, so the approach doesn't change the number. The 3280's face is flat and floor-standing with no knee clearance underneath, so a visitor in a wheelchair makes a parallel approach and reaches sideways — still 48″.

48″ is a ceiling, not a target

Designing at the maximum satisfies the letter of the standard and serves tall standing adults. It fails seated visitors and children, who are exactly the people the reach range exists for.

Rev 1 target — 38″ AFF

  • 10″ below the ADA maximum. That margin absorbs mount tolerance, floor variation and the fact that nobody has built this yet.
  • At or just above elbow height for a shorter standing adult, and a natural reach-down for a taller one.
  • Comfortably inside a seated visitor's reach, and reachable by a child of about eight.
It improves the screen too

Buttons at 38″ put the 24″ screen centre at 51.5″. Adult seated eye height is roughly 47–51″, so the screen is very nearly centred on a seated visitor, while a standing adult looks down about 11° — normal reading posture. The ADA-driven placement is better for everyone than the 40″ this study first drew.

What it costs the mount

With the buttons at 38″ the kiosk's bottom edge sits at 34.75″ AFF — above the 27″ threshold, so §307.2 caps the projection into a circulation path at 4″. With the recommended 3 mm ACM face (§06) the enclosure is 3.25″ deep, leaving 0.75″ for the mounting adapter — and a plywood face instead would cut that to 0.5″.

Worth asking, not worth assuming

§307.2 exists to stop someone with a cane walking into an object their sweep misses. The 3280 is floor-standing and cane-detectable, and a visitor is stopped by the cabinet before the kiosk is in reach — so the hazard the rule targets may not apply here. That is the museum's accessibility coordinator's call, not ours. Design the adapter to 4″ total, and ask.

Placement math

The button centreline sits 3.25″ above the bottom of the enclosure, and the door face runs from about 3″ to 71.15″ AFF. So for a button height B and a kiosk height KH:

kiosk bottom = B − 3.25″
kiosk top = B + KH − 3.25″ ≤ 71.15″
→ B ≤ 74.40″ − KH

Correction to the earlier study

With the buttons at a properly-chosen 38″ rather than the 40″ first drawn, the 32″ fits — its top lands at 70.15″, an inch under the door. It is not a geometric failure and this study should not have called it one.

What the 32″ actually costs is freedom. It clips half the comfort band away, and the remaining inch of headroom is inside normal build tolerance. The case against it now rests on weight and proportion alone — §08.

The enclosure by itself. Everything in this drawing is buildable and testable before the 3280 is involved at all.

Sheet R1-02 · kiosk front elevation
Sheet R1-03 · button plate detail

Why a separate button plate

The buttons are the highest-wear part of the exhibit and the only part a visitor touches. Splitting them onto their own plate decouples switch-hole tolerance from the big face plate, lets the whole control assembly be built and tested on the bench with a jumper to the Pi, and means a change of switch style recuts a $15 plate instead of the face.

30 mm anti-vandal stainless, momentary, 30.5 mm hole. A big public target, industrial life, about $10 each, and a hole size so standard that any replacement in 2033 will fit. Labels engraved into the plate — stickers peel.

Flat, not angled. An angled lower surface adds a bevelled joint, adds depth at the bottom, and complicates the mount's load path. At 40″ AFF a flat face is comfortable for a standing adult. Revisit only if observation says otherwise.

No fasteners on the visitor-facing surface. The plate is captured from behind — nothing on the front is a screw a bored twelve-year-old can turn.

04Side section

Depth decides how far the kiosk stands off the machine — every eighth of an inch here is visible, and ADA §307.2 caps the whole projection at 4″. Drawn with the recommended 3 mm ACM face (§06); a plywood face costs another 3/8″.

Sheet R1-04 · side section · depth to 3.5″ target · mounting interface left blank by intent

What forces the depth

  • The monitor's thickest point — 1.6″ to 2.3″. The only hard number in the stack, and the reason depth grows with screen size even though the panel doesn't.
  • Cable bend at the connectors — 0.75″ to 1.75″. The one you can design away, and the difference between 3.5″ and 4.5″. Route HDMI and DC downward into the button bay with right-angle plugs.
  • Face plate + rear panel. A 3 mm ACM face and a 1/2″ ply rear come to 0.62″ together. A plywood face instead makes it 1.0″ — and that 3/8″ comes straight out of the mounting adapter's budget.

What does not

  • The Raspberry Pi. 0.65″ tall, and it does not go behind the monitor. It goes in the button bay on a slide-out tray, where 4″ of depth is otherwise doing nothing. Design the enclosure around fitting a Pi behind the panel and you will build it an inch too deep.
Reversed from earlier work

The monitor stays cased. De-casing buys about 1.2″ and costs the EMI shielding, the thermal design, the power board's own enclosure, and any hope of a volunteer replacing it. Not worth it.

Readability governs, resolution doesn't

The real constraint is cap height. Comfortable reading at 6 ft wants roughly 0.35″ of cap height — about a half-inch em. On the 24″, 20.74″ tall across 1920 px, that is a 46 px body size, giving about 47 characters per line. A good measure for the 3–5 bullets per screen the content bar allows.

The 22″ lands at ~42 characters. Workable, but it starts to constrain the writing. All four are legible at 3–6 ft; a 1080p 24″ at 93 ppi is crisp at arm's length and dead sharp at exhibit distance. Don't pay for 1440p unless the salvage pile hands it to you.

06What it's made of, and where to buy it

Three ways to build the box. What separates them isn't strength and it isn't cost — it's whether the finished thing looks bought or looks homemade.

First: what these materials actually are

ACM

aluminium composite · “Dibond”

Two thin sheets of aluminium glued either side of a plastic core — a metal sandwich about 1/8″ thick. Stiff, dead flat, and the colour is baked onto the aluminium at the factory. Nothing to sand, nothing to paint. Almost every storefront sign and road sign you've ever looked at is this stuff.

HPL

high-pressure laminate · “Formica”

The material on a kitchen counter. Here's the catch: it is not a board. It's a 1/32″ skin with no stiffness of its own, so it has to be glued down onto plywood or MDF first. That gluing step is the word “bonded” doing a lot of quiet work.

Compact laminate

solid phenolic · Trespa, Fundermax

The same material as Formica, but pressed thick enough to stand on its own — 1/4″ to 1/2″. Restroom partitions and school lab benches. Beautiful and effectively permanent, but heavy and mostly sold in architectural quantities.

Baltic birch

void-free plywood

Very good plywood — many thin layers, no hidden gaps inside. Strong, and it machines beautifully. But it is wood: to look finished it wants sanding, sealing and spraying, and that is where volunteer builds usually come unstuck.

The move that actually makes this easy

Don't buy a sheet. Send the file.

SendCutSend will CNC-route the face plate and the button plate out of 3 mm matte black ACM and mail them to you. You upload a drawing file, they cut it to ±0.005″, and it ships in 2–4 days.

Their maximum part is 30″ × 44″. Our face plate is 15.4 × 28.7 and the button plate is 12.9 × 4.0 — both fit comfortably, and they'd nest on a single order.

This takes the hardest job away from the volunteer crew entirely: no CNC access needed at CDL, no 4 × 8 sheet to buy and mostly throw away, no jig for the big screen window, and the three button holes arrive already cut to size. What's left is building a plywood box — which is the part any woodworker gets right the first time.

One small thing that decides the material

Panel-mount buttons have a maximum panel thickness — how thick a face they can clamp through. It's model-specific, commonly somewhere between 6 and 11 mm.

3 mm ACM clears every one of them. A 1/2″ plywood-and-Formica face is 12.7 mm — at or over the limit for most switches, which means counterboring the back of all three holes by hand. Small detail, real annoyance, and it points the same way as everything else.

Two limits on ACM worth knowing

It can't be tapped or bent. Fastening goes through clearance holes into threaded inserts in the plywood cleats — which was already the plan.

Cut edges show the black plastic core. On a black panel that's invisible. On white it's a visible dark line — one more reason to order it black on both sides.

Cutting the larger size is the safe direction, but confirm it against the datasheet of the switch you actually buy — it's the only dimension here tied to a part nobody owns yet.

Why the face plate is safe to cut before the monitor arrives

The screen window is 0.25″ oversize per side, on purpose. “24 inch” covers 23.6″–24.0″ diagonals and this window clears every one of them without cropping a pixel — and the margin falls on the monitor's own black bezel, so behind a black face plate nothing shows.

The monitor is aligned to the window, not the other way round: it hangs on a VESA plate on an internal frame, so whatever offset a particular model has between its panel and its VESA pattern gets taken up there.

Labels

There's no text in the cut files. BACK / HOME / NEXT is a separate step, and the native answer for ACM is cut vinyl from a sign shop — this is sign material, and it's how every road sign in the country is lettered. Durable, cheap, replaceable. Ask SendCutSend whether they'll engrave ACM first; if they will, that's better still.

07Attachment interface

Deliberately not designed. The enclosure's job here is to have a flat back and a known weight — that is enough for an adapter to be designed against later, and designing it now would be guessing.

Sheet R1-05 · rear · mounting interface zone, design TBD

Constraints recorded now, so the enclosure doesn't foreclose them

  • Reversible, non-destructive, removable, visually discreet. No holes, no cuts, no permanent modification to the door or the cabinet. This is a hard requirement, not a preference — the 3280 is a museum artifact.
  • Must carry the kiosk in shear plus the moment from a centre-of-mass roughly 1.6″ off the door, with a safety factor for someone leaning on it.
  • Total projection from the door face should stay at or under 4″ (§307.2 — the kiosk's leading edge is at 34.75″ AFF, above the 27″ threshold). The enclosure is 3.25″, so the adapter's budget is 0.75″.
  • Candidates, unevaluated: edge brackets hooking the door's return flange; compression straps around the cabinet; a full-door backing plate captured top and bottom; clamps on existing door hardware.
  • Anything that loads the original hinges has to be checked against them.
This is why weight matters

The mount is undesigned and unconstrained. 23 lb of strap-and-bracket load on a hollow sheet-metal door is a solvable problem. 34 lb is where you start having to transfer load to the cabinet frame — and the mount stops being simple. That, more than proportion, is the argument against the 32″.

08Recommendation

Rev 1

24″-class display, 23.8″ actual. Finished kiosk 15-3/8″ W × 28-11/16″ H × 3-1/4″ D, about 23 lb.

Buttons 38″ AFF · kiosk bottom 34.75″ · kiosk top 63.44″ · screen centre 51.5″ · 7.71″ headroom · 3 mm ACM face on a Baltic birch box

Proportion is the deciding argument. At 15.4″ wide the kiosk is 63% of the door — clearly an added object, clearly not trying to be the machine. At 70% and 79% the 27″ and 32″ start to read as a monitor wearing a cabinet, which is precisely the impression a museum exhibit should not give.

Weight is the second argument, and it may be the more important one — see section 06. The mount has to carry this off a hollow door without a single fastener into the artifact.

Availability closes it. 24″ 1080p IPS is the highest-volume monitor ever made. It is the most likely thing in the warehouse, the cheapest thing to buy, and the easiest thing for a docent to replace in 2033 without calling anyone.

If a 27″ falls out of the salvage pile

Take it. It isn't a bad answer — it's a slightly worse answer, and the enclosure is parametric enough that switching costs one face plate. Budget the extra 4 lb into the mount. 22″ is the fallback if the mount turns out harder than expected; 20 lb is genuinely easy to hang. 32″ is out — but not for the reason this study first gave. With the buttons properly placed at 38″ it fits the door with an inch to spare (§02). It goes because of the weight, the proportion, and the VA-panel risk: visitors approach an exhibit from the side, and a VA panel at 45° looks bad in exactly that situation. It also leaves an inch of headroom against build tolerance and clips half the comfortable button band away.

Two go/no-go criteria on any candidate monitor

1 · It must power itself back on after a mains cut. The exhibit runs on an AC timer. Many monitors return to standby and need a button press — that's a black screen every morning until a docent notices. Test before committing: switched strip, kill power, restore, confirm it comes back with no input.

2 · Matte only. Overhead lights and daylight. Glossy panels mirror them.

Sequence

  1. Choose the monitor. Buy or salvage; run the power-cut test. Gate — everything downstream is dimensioned off this.
  2. Build the enclosure. Bench, no 3280 involved.
  3. Install monitor, Pi, buttons, wiring, software.
  4. Bench-test as a complete kiosk. Run it for a week on a table.
  5. Then design and build the reversible mounting adapter.
  6. Rev 2, if desired, revisits the replacement-door concept.

Steps 1–4 have no dependency on the 3280 at all. C1 — the closing clearance that gated the entire previous design — no longer gates anything, and neither does the door height, the aperture, or the inner perforated panel.
